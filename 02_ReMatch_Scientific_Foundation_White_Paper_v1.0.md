# Re.Match Scientific Foundation White Paper v1.0

**Generated:** 2026-05-07  
**Status:** scientific foundation and positioning synthesis  
**Purpose:** defensible research-backed explanation of why Re.Match is plausible, useful, measurable, and aligned with evidence-based behavioral-health and reintegration practice

## Executive thesis

Re.Match is best understood as a **behavioral-health navigation, recovery-capital amplification, and reintegration intelligence system**. It does not deliver therapy, diagnosis, legal advice, medical treatment, or criminal-risk prediction. It improves the user’s ability to find, qualify for, sequence, and act on the human services and evidence-based supports already available in the environment.

The intervention target is **information poverty**: the gap between the existence of services and a person’s practical ability to discover, understand, access, and persist through them.

For people experiencing addiction recovery, homelessness, reentry, poverty, and mental-health instability, service access is not a simple information problem. It is a constraint problem. Feasibility depends on documents, eligibility, transportation, legal restrictions, cost, disability, readiness, language, digital access, trauma history, mental-health symptoms, cognitive load, stigma, waitlists, and support. Re.Match treats these as first-class variables.

## 1. Recovery-oriented foundation

SAMHSA defines recovery as a process of change through which people improve health and wellness, live self-directed lives, and strive to reach their full potential [S1]. SAMHSA identifies four major dimensions of recovery: health, home, purpose, and community [S2].

Re.Match maps directly onto this model:

| Recovery dimension | Re.Match translation |
|---|---|
| Health | treatment, MOUD/MAUD routing, mental-health care, primary care, benefits, crisis supports |
| Home | shelter, transitional housing, recovery housing, permanent housing, documents, benefits |
| Purpose | employment, education, credentials, volunteering, creative pursuits, skill development |
| Community | peer support, mentors, support groups, social prescribing, family repair, prosocial belonging |

This means Re.Match is not an abstinence-only directory or a compliance checklist. It is a recovery-oriented navigation system that helps users build the conditions of recovery.

## 2. Recovery capital as the central mechanism

Recovery capital refers to the resources a person can draw on to initiate and sustain recovery. Re.Match operationalizes recovery capital by converting a user’s life context into a plan for increasing practical, social, psychological, institutional, and community supports.

| Recovery-capital domain | Re.Match mechanism |
|---|---|
| Human capital | education, therapy, executive-function scaffolding, treatment access, skills, health coverage |
| Social capital | peers, mentors, support groups, family repair, prosocial networks |
| Physical capital | housing, food, clothing, IDs, phone access, transportation, benefits |
| Community capital | clinics, legal aid, recovery organizations, community colleges, workforce boards, libraries |
| Cultural capital | identity-respecting supports, language access, lived-experience resources, culturally relevant services |
| Purpose capital | meaningful roles, service, work, school, creativity, athletics, community contribution |

The system’s technical function is to locate and sequence these assets.

## 3. CHIME and personal mental-health recovery

The CHIME framework organizes personal recovery around Connectedness, Hope, Identity, Meaning, and Empowerment [S15]. Re.Match uses CHIME not as decoration, but as product architecture.

| CHIME domain | Product mechanism |
|---|---|
| Connectedness | match to peer support, groups, mentors, recovery communities, community activity |
| Hope | show realistic next steps, fallback options, and visible pathways out of paralysis |
| Identity | help users move toward roles such as worker, student, parent, builder, artist, athlete, helper |
| Meaning | match to purpose-aligned education, employment, volunteering, creativity, service |
| Empowerment | preserve choice, explain recommendations, let users select goals and pace |

This is why the dossier must be more than a service list. It must help the user see a plausible future self.

## 4. Trauma-informed design

SAMHSA describes trauma-informed systems as those that realize trauma’s widespread impact, recognize signs of trauma, integrate trauma knowledge into policies and practice, and resist retraumatization [S3]. Re.Match converts this into design requirements:

- minimize unnecessary data collection;
- explain why sensitive questions are asked;
- avoid coercive and shaming language;
- preserve user choice;
- offer fallback options;
- include peer and human supports;
- flag crisis situations for human escalation;
- protect privacy;
- avoid punitive risk-scoring;
- distinguish verified facts from estimates and uncertainty.

## 5. Addiction neuroscience and recovery environment design

NIDA explains that addiction affects reward, stress, decision-making, planning, and impulse-control systems [S4]. Substance-use disorders are also associated with impairments in attention, inhibition, working memory, and decision-making [S19]. Longitudinal alcohol-recovery literature indicates that neuropsychological recovery can vary by function and timeframe [S20].

Therefore, Re.Match should avoid exaggerated claims about a single universal neuroplasticity window. The defensible claim is stronger:

> Early recovery is a high-leverage neurobehavioral transition period in which environment, reinforcement, stress, treatment access, routine, social context, and executive-function scaffolding can influence recovery trajectories.

Re.Match supports recovery indirectly by helping users assemble recovery-supportive conditions:

- medication and treatment access;
- peer support;
- housing stability;
- prosocial routines;
- substance-free rewards;
- employment and education;
- transportation;
- crisis planning;
- meaningful goals.

The system does not claim to directly change the brain. It improves access to the environmental and service conditions under which evidence-based recovery is more likely to occur.

## 6. Evidence-based treatment routing

Re.Match should route users toward evidence-based human services while preserving clinical boundaries.

### MOUD

CDC states that medication treatment for opioid use disorder is associated with reduced overdose risk and overall mortality and identifies buprenorphine, methadone, and naltrexone as FDA-approved medications [S5]. FDA also describes those medications as safe and effective for OUD treatment [S6].

### Co-occurring disorders

SAMHSA defines co-occurring disorders as the coexistence of mental health and substance use disorders [S7]. SAMHSA also states that integrated care is recommended and can improve outcomes such as psychiatric symptoms, substance use, quality of life, housing stability, and arrests [S8].

### Contingency management

SAMHSA’s contingency management advisory describes the evidence base and funding guardrails for using incentives in substance-use treatment [S10]. Re.Match does not deliver contingency management, but can help locate programs that offer it.

### Peer recovery support

SAMHSA describes peer support workers as helping people become and stay engaged in recovery and reduce relapse likelihood [S35]. Re.Match can identify local peer workers, peer-led recovery organizations, mutual-aid groups, and recovery community organizations.

## 7. Reentry, Central Eight, desistance, and Good Lives Model

The Risk-Need-Responsivity model remains influential in correctional rehabilitation and emphasizes matching intensity and services to risk, needs, and responsivity [S13]. However, a 2024 umbrella review found the evidence base for some RNR principles to be mixed and often low quality [S14]. Re.Match should therefore use RNR cautiously as an organizing scaffold, not as a deterministic prediction system.

The project reframes Central Eight-style domains from deficits into development domains. Instead of asking only “what risk should be controlled,” it asks “what life domain can be built.”

The Good Lives Model strengthens this by emphasizing capabilities, strengths, and prosocial routes to valued human outcomes [S16]. Desistance research emphasizes identity, agency, social bonds, stable work, and movement toward valued adult roles [S29].

Grant-safe formulation:

> Re.Match uses reentry science to organize dynamic life domains relevant to reintegration, while maintaining a development-oriented and non-punitive posture.

## 8. Housing and basic needs as behavioral-health infrastructure

HUD’s 2024 AHAR reported more than 770,000 people experiencing homelessness on a single night in January 2024, an 18 percent increase from 2023 [S11, S12].

For Re.Match, housing is not merely a service category. It is a behavioral-health platform. Without a safe place to sleep, preserve medication, maintain hygiene, receive mail, attend appointments, comply with court obligations, and regulate stress, recovery and mental health are structurally undermined.

Re.Match therefore treats basic needs as Tier Zero:

1. safety tonight;
2. food and hygiene;
3. identification/documents;
4. health coverage;
5. phone/internet access;
6. transportation;
7. treatment linkage;
8. peer support;
9. employment/education;
10. long-term purpose path.

## 9. Employment, education, and meaningful role restoration

SAMHSA’s purpose dimension includes meaningful daily activities such as work, school, volunteerism, family caretaking, and creative endeavors [S2]. IPS supported employment meta-analysis shows improved competitive employment outcomes among participants receiving IPS compared with usual services [S17].

Re.Match connects employment and education to mental-health recovery by routing users toward:

- GED/high-school completion;
- community college;
- trade programs;
- apprenticeships;
- reentry-friendly employers;
- workforce boards;
- vocational rehabilitation;
- IPS supported employment;
- paid training pipelines;
- volunteer-to-employment pathways.

## 10. Social prescribing and behavioral activation

Social prescribing links people to voluntary and community-sector activities and services to address social determinants of health and wellbeing [S18]. Behavioral activation approaches target substance-free reward and value-consistent activity in SUD contexts [S34].

Re.Match’s social-prescribing layer should identify:

- gyms and physical training;
- martial arts;
- outdoor groups;
- maker spaces;
- art, music, and writing groups;
- volunteering;
- peer communities;
- faith-neutral community organizations;
- study groups;
- civic service;
- recovery-friendly recreational structures.

This supports the principle that recovery requires replacement rewards, identity, belonging, and activity, not only restraint.

## 11. Cognitive load and executive-function scaffolding

Re.Match’s dossier can function as an executive-function scaffold. It reduces:

- search burden;
- decision burden;
- sequencing burden;
- paperwork burden;
- eligibility burden;
- transportation burden;
- follow-up burden;
- rejection/fallback burden.

For users experiencing executive-function strain, trauma, withdrawal, depression, anxiety, ADHD, unstable sleep, or crisis overload, a structured plan can act as a cognitive prosthetic.

## 12. Implementation-science native architecture

RE-AIM evaluates Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance [S21, S22]. CFIR helps identify real-world implementation barriers and facilitators [S23]. EPIS structures implementation across Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, and Sustainment [S24]. Proctor’s implementation outcomes include acceptability, adoption, appropriateness, feasibility, fidelity, cost, penetration, and sustainability [S25, S26]. Measurement-based care provides a model for repeated outcome monitoring [S27].

Re.Match should be evaluated both as an individual navigation intervention and as organizational infrastructure.

## 13. Scientific positioning statement

Re.Match is a precision navigation layer for people whose recovery and reintegration are blocked by fragmented information, unstable resources, and high cognitive load. It is grounded in recovery science, mental-health recovery, addiction neuroscience, trauma-informed care, social determinants of health, reentry science, housing/employment stabilization, social prescribing, and implementation science. Its innovation is not AI search alone. Its innovation is constraint-aware synthesis: turning a user’s life context into a feasible, evidence-aligned, action-ready roadmap.
